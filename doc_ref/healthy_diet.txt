WHY DIET MATTERS
What people eat strongly affects their health. A healthy diet across life helps prevent malnutrition and non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, heart disease, stroke and cancer. Eating habits formed in childhood often continue into adulthood. Food must also be safe, meaning free of harmful germs and chemicals.

FOUR CORE PRINCIPLES
- Adequacy: enough nutrients to prevent deficiencies, without going over what is needed
- Balance: energy eaten matches energy used, with a sensible mix of protein, fat and carbohydrate
- Moderation: limit foods and ingredients that can harm health
- Diversity: eat a wide variety of nutritious foods within and across food groups
The exact make-up of a healthy diet varies with age, gender, activity level, culture and locally available foods.

FOOD VARIETY AND OVERALL PATTERN
A mix of minimally processed foods works best: fruits, vegetables, pulses (lentils, chickpeas, beans), whole grains, nuts and lean sources of protein. Diverse diets make it more likely that vitamin and mineral needs are met. Many adults may benefit from more plant-based protein, especially in place of red meat. Highly processed foods, which are often high in sodium, sugar or unhealthy fat, are linked to poorer health.

FRUITS AND VEGETABLES
- People over 10 years old should aim for at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day.
- Children 2-5 years: at least 250 g; children 6-9 years: at least 350 g.
- Fresh is great; frozen and canned are fine if no sugar or excess salt is added.
- Fruit juice, even without added sugar, contains free sugars, so intake should be limited.

WHOLE GRAINS, CARBOHYDRATES AND FIBRE
- For most people, unrefined carbohydrates should provide roughly 45-75% of daily energy, mainly from whole grains (e.g. oats, brown rice, millet, wheat, maize), vegetables, fruits and pulses.
- Fibre: at least 25 g a day for people over 10; at least 15 g (ages 2-5) or 21 g (ages 6-9) for younger children.

BALANCED MEALS - OTHER KEY GUIDANCE
Sugars:
- Keep free sugars below 10% of daily energy (about 50 g, or around 12 level teaspoons, on a 2000-calorie diet). Below 5% may give extra benefit.
- Free sugars include sugars added to food and drink, and sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice.
Fats:
- Fat should be about 15% to 30% of daily energy for adults.
- Prefer unsaturated fats (fish, avocado, nuts, and oils such as olive, sunflower, soybean, canola) over saturated fats (fatty meat, butter, ghee, cream, cheese, palm and coconut oil).
- Saturated fat under 10% of energy; trans fat under 1%. Industrially produced trans fat should be avoided.
- Steaming or boiling instead of frying helps reduce fat.
Protein:
- About 10-15% of energy is usually enough for adults (roughly 50-75 g on a 2000-calorie diet). Can come from both plant and animal sources.
Salt and potassium:
- Adults should eat less than 5 g of salt (2 g sodium) per day.
- Much salt comes from processed foods, bread, and condiments such as soy sauce, stock cubes and fish sauce.
- Potassium of at least 3510 mg/day for adults may help; fruits and vegetables are good sources.
Vitamins and minerals:
- Around 30 essential micronutrients are needed in small amounts. A varied diet (fruit, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, lean animal foods) usually supplies them. Examples: beans or lean meat for iron, dark green leafy vegetables for vitamin A, iodized salt for iodine.

INFANTS AND YOUNG CHILDREN
- Breastfeed exclusively for the first 6 months, and continue to 2 years and beyond.
- From 6 months, add varied, safe, nutrient-dense foods, including animal-source foods, fruit and vegetables. Do not add salt or sugar. Avoid sweet drinks and foods high in sugar, salt or trans fat.
