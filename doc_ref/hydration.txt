WHAT DEHYDRATION IS
Dehydration happens when the body loses more fluid than it takes in, so it no longer has enough fluid to work properly. It can be mild, or serious enough to be life-threatening.

COMMON CAUSES
- Diarrhea or vomiting
- Heavy sweating
- Passing too much urine (can be due to some medicines or illnesses)
- Fever
- Not drinking enough water or other fluids

WHO IS MORE LIKELY TO BECOME DEHYDRATED
- Older adults, because some lose their sense of thirst with age
- Infants and young children, who often have diarrhea or vomiting
- People with long-term conditions that increase urination or sweating (for example diabetes, cystic fibrosis or kidney problems)
- People taking medicines that increase urination or sweating
- People who exercise or work outdoors in hot weather

COMMON SYMPTOMS
In adults:
- Feeling very thirsty
- Dry mouth
- Urinating and sweating less than usual
- Dark-colored urine
- Dry skin
- Tiredness
- Dizziness

In infants and young children:
- Dry mouth and tongue
- Crying without tears
- No wet diapers for 3 hours or more
- High fever
- Unusual sleepiness or drowsiness
- Irritability
- Sunken-looking eyes

WARNING SIGNS - GET MEDICAL HELP RIGHT AWAY
Seek urgent medical care if dehydration symptoms come with any of these:
- Confusion
- Fainting
- No urination
- Rapid heartbeat
- Rapid breathing
- Shock

PREVENTION AND FLUID NEEDS
- Drink enough water every day. Fluid needs differ from person to person, so a health care provider can advise how much is right for you. (The source does not give a single fixed daily amount.)
- Limit drinks that contain sugar and caffeine.
- Drink extra fluids in hot weather, especially when exercising or working outside.
- Sports drinks can help if a lot of minerals are lost in sweat, but many are high in sugar, so do not overuse them.
- Drink extra fluids when sick. If it is hard to keep liquids down, take small sips of water or suck on ice chips.

DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT
- A provider checks symptoms and vital signs and does a physical exam. Blood tests (electrolytes such as sodium and potassium, kidney function) and urine tests may be used.
- Treatment is replacing lost fluids and electrolytes. Mild cases may only need plenty of water. Sports drinks may help replace electrolytes, and over-the-counter oral rehydration solutions exist for children.
- Severe cases may need intravenous (IV) fluids with salt in a hospital.
